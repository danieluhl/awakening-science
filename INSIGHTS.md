
# Personal Learnings from Dan

Most of what I share elsewhere parrots sources I've found valuable, but this
book is mine. This means it's rough, not well groomed or thought through, but
it's also unique content you'll find nowhere else in the world. I hope it's more
of a diamond in the rough than a lump of coal for you.

# What is "awakening / nirvana / enlightenment"?

There is no magic "awakening" to chase; being aware of the current moment _is_
the goal.

It is accessible to anyone in nearly any moment. Pause and look around. Chances
are, even with no meditation, insight practice, or any other training, you just
had a moment of "awakening," or whatever you want to call it. The practice is to
have those moments more often.

There are two ways of saying the same goal:

1. "Increase the quality of attention"
2. "Increase the frequency of high-quality attention"

High-quality attention is when we have the right level of attention (on the
broad-to-focused spectrum) on what is real, true, impactful, and helpful.

Increasing the frequency of high-quality attention simply means noticing, often,
what the quality of your attention is, and adjusting to improve it.

# What is practice?

When I was first practicing meditation, I was on retreat and decided I would
meet with the teacher. In one sitting, I made up a story in my head. I noticed
my hands would go numb and tingle. The sensation was fantastic. _I told myself_
I was well on my way to enlightenment and sitting so well so far. I decided to
tell the teacher about my hands and how it felt. I imagined him praising my
practice: "You are doing so well, you're very far on the path, here is some
further insight to really push you onward!" The time came for me to meet with
him, and I relayed my sensation experience. His reply was simply, "If you can't
feel your hands, wiggle them a little, they are still there." This completely
deflated me, but I'll never forget the lesson: practice is about practice, not
whatever story you're telling yourself about the practice.

Meditating on a cushion in silence with other people is not a performance; it's
_practice_. It's what I call _easy mode_. Fellow video game players will be
familiar with the various modes many games have. On _easy mode_, players are put
in a position where they basically can't fail. The goal is to run through the
game and experience the story.

We sit up so we can practice and avoid nodding off. We sit still so we can be
calm enough to notice the present moment: this is the practice. We sit with
others because it pushes our social and mental state to accept the practice. We
sit in silence so as not to distract from the practice.

This is easy mode. And yet the thoughts pour in to distract in every way. Which
is perfectly fine. As soon as we _notice_ the thoughts, _that_ moment of
noticing _is_ success in the practice. Now start again.

If you practice better standing, or sitting on a chair, or lying down with a
bright light on, great, do that! In my opinion, all meditation centers should
allow any person to practice in the way that works best for them. But don't
deceive yourself about what is working and what is not. And go in understanding
that for thousands of years, sitting on a cushion cross-legged in silence with
eyes closed, with people around, has been working, so I'd at least give it a
try.

# Why do we practice?

Practice is about practice: not about how to live life, not about attaining
nirvana, not about feeling tingles, not about feeling pain.

I started practicing with vipassana, a specific body-scanning technique. When
practicing vipassana, the point is to practice. If you're practicing focused
attention on the breath, do that. If you're practicing moving awareness from
focused to broad and back, do that. Anything else that happens can happen, and
that's perfectly fine. But the time of practice is for practice. There's no need
to search for insight; that's just another distraction.

Think of it like practicing piano. You slow it down and make it as easy as
possible, then through repetition gradually speed up.

# The Path

There is an explosion that happens downstream of practice. Each level multiplies
the previous. And you can jump around through these. I imagine a million-path
corn maze where each person is guaranteed to take their own route, but everyone
hits roughly similar points along the way.

## Practice

Practice at this level will be largely the same for everyone.

Practice is simply about the quality of attention and thought in the current
moment.

## Insights

From practice spring forth 1,000 insights (logical, philosophical, and
biological implications). This is where a good teacher will jump in after a day
of meditation to give some instruction on what insights practitioners of the
past have realized. This can speed up this level.

This will be different for each person, but there are some great common, generic
insights that can be transmitted via stories or morals. I've found the most
potent and common ones are:

- everything changes
- suffering = intensity (pleasure / discomfort) × reaction (grasping / aversion)
- learn to want/do what you already have/do

## Integration

From each insight spring forth 1,000 shifts and modifications to our behavior
and perception of day-to-day activity. Think of these like fine-tuning the
nervous system.

Most of these shifts are very subtle and happen over time. However, some are
more obvious. For example, someone with significant trauma in their past may
have a kind of "kink" in the nerves (metaphorically). This can feel like a kind
of blockage of energy (metaphorically/experientially). Through insights, that
block may finally be found, accepted, integrated into being, fully welcomed into
the moment, and, with time and work, fully resolved.

Note that reliving trauma is not generally helpful, but unearthing otherwise
hidden trauma can be. Thinking through past trauma is like reliving a car
accident; we'd rather be like the mechanic looking at the damage and preparing
the repairs. This means looking at our current underlying goals, defaults, and
intuitions, and surgically adjusting them to fit our insights.

This will be almost entirely unique to the individual. If someone else is
telling their story at this level, listen politely, but don't expect to share a
path here. You're not going to, or coming from, the same place.

# It's easy, actually, and you can do it.

I feel awkward when someone compliments my beard. I literally did _less_ work to
grow it (I skipped shaving). Am I deserving of such praise for something I put
zero effort into? Something I was born with and over which I have no control?

My brother has been vegan for many years. Some time ago we were talking about
it, and I said something like, "I'm not sure I could do that," and I'll never
forget his reply: "Yeah, you can. Just don't eat meat."

It's doing less, the opposite of effort, yet commendable.

Similarly, a great meditation practice is one where nothing happens. No
movement, no thought, just sitting and attending to what arises.

And here's the insight I eventually arrived at: what if this is true for
everything we do in life? What if we could get rewarded with great financial
wealth for doing less? What if we could get healthier by doing less work? What
if the only thing standing in the path of everything we've ever wanted is the
great effort that we expend to get it?

Okay, this is ridiculous. Or is it?

Yes, it is; you have to work hard for what you want.

Or do you?

Yes, you definitely do. Health is a great example. Eating healthy, meditating,
working out, getting good sleep: all require effort.

Okay... let's dig in and take those one at a time:

1. Eat healthy. What would be the optimal way to eat healthier: A. eat less junk
   food, or B. eat more vegetables? What are the chances that you would enjoy
   eating more vegetables if you eat less junk food? Have you ever been hiking,
   exhausted, underfed, body starving for calories, and had a very healthy meal
   with no junk? Was it enjoyable and healthy?

   So here it's pretty clear: applying less effort (eating less junk food)
   works.

2. Meditate. Easy win: meditation is one of the least effortful things you can
   possibly do. In fact, the less effort you put into the practice, the better
   you do. Here I'm not counting determination, resolve, or similar. But the
   very nature of the practice is letting experience come to you.

3. Work out. Okay, gotcha, can't work out without more effort, by definition,
   right?!

   Once I was on the Peloton playing their game Lanebreak (an excellent game, by
   the way). In Lanebreak there are several places where you have a decision: go
   hard or go easy. I noticed a thought coming to mind: _can_ I give more effort
   on the upcoming block? How hard can I push? And having a bit of a mind/body
   separation experience, I looked down and thought, how hard can this body push
   in this moment?

   My mind became a flurry of logic: there are physical limits, but there are
   also mental walls I put around my exercise. There are reasonable expectations
   around my workout, downstream repercussions. If I push too hard now, then on
   the next turn I may not go as far. What is the net gain of pushing so hard?
   Maybe I'm already pushing harder than I _should_. "Should" is an interesting
   word here: how hard _should_ I be pushing? How much of each turn of the
   pedals is _wrong_? What is the heuristic of what I will do, whether or not I
   _should_ be doing it?

   In the next moment everything dropped away, and one phrase came to my mind
   that I began to see in all decisions in life: "Everything is either
   _inevitable_ or _impossible_; there is no worry either way."

   In that moment I looked at my legs pumping and smiled. They're doing great,
   and it's absolutely, undoubtedly _effortless_! I was putting no more effort
   into my legs than I put into breathing or beating my own heart. What the body
   does will be what the body does. This isn't dissociation; it's being fully in
   the body, letting it be what it is and do what it does. And this goes far
   beyond just the body.

4. Sleep, I think, really drives the point home here and can be used as an easy
   metaphor for every part of life: the harder you chase it, the further away it
   gets.

   Some good advice for falling asleep: try as hard as you can to stay awake.

   Similarly, if you are feeling tired in a meditation practice, welcome the
   sleepy mind openly and notice it. Chances are you'll soon be more aware and
   awake.

This little exercise has yet to fail me. Try it on yard work and gardening (it's
silly to "try" hard to grow plants), preparing coffee, cleaning the house, all
the parenting things. I could go on and on.

## Try hard

In gaming there's a very old and very common label: a "try-hard," or someone
going into "try-hard" mode. Originally this was someone who clearly wanted to be
something they were not. But over time this became a kind of mode that anyone,
even the best gamers, could embody. It's when you stop playing your intuitive,
practiced game and overanalyze and push with effort on every aspect of the game.
You panic and sweat over every detail and as a consequence play a considerably
worse game.

Now, I will concede that _if_ someone, or more likely an AI, were able to truly
analyze all aspects and heuristics of a game, they could and would play in full
try-hard mode and _always_ win. However, and this is another gem of an insight
from meditation, there is no possible way for the human mind to analyze,
categorize, label, measure, or understand fully even the briefest moment of
experience. And if we can't even do this for a single moment, what makes us
think we could get a handle on this for a full second, or minute, or over years?
It would be cool, but it's simply preposterous.

## What is a thought?

And yet the ego will try. But what is trying? How do we "try hard"? The answer I
found is simple: with thoughts. There's a great way to tell what is experience
in the moment and what is a "thought":

If you can describe it in words, it's a thought.

This is beautiful and universally true. Any moment of experience is
indescribable if you really look.

Now, it's also true that, in a way, everything is a thought. That is, any piece
of conscious experience is "made" of the same substance. More specifically, our
brain constructs reality in each moment in a way that is evolutionarily
optimized for humans to exist and pass on our genes.

But not all thoughts are equal, so here I'm separating direct experience
thoughts from conceptual thoughts, fantasy, or any other sorts of thoughts that
spontaneously arise but are not based on direct incoming data in the moment.

A good way to picture this is with two mirrors bouncing an image back and forth,
where each reflection can slightly warp the image. Direct experience is our very
human reflection on incoming data: sight, sound, taste, or any of the other
twenty-some human senses delivering direct information to the construction of
consciousness. Any reflections beyond that initial construction can be
considered to be thoughts. These are the thoughts that can warp and distort
reality to make a story around it. They build futures and pasts and create
continuity around time and memories and purpose and everything that makes us.
Obviously these thoughts are also necessary and proven out as useful via
evolution.

As a quick aside: towards the end of a meditation retreat, a student asked,
"When do we take the thought path to enlightenment, and when do we dismiss the
thought and return attention to the breath?" The teacher didn't understand the
question, and we moved on to other things, but the question stuck with me. As I
stated before, practice is not for enlightenment; it's for practice. So my
initial answer is, never invite the thoughts to tea while practicing. I still
think this is correct. If it's important, it will come up again in another
context; you won't forget to follow the thought path when the time is right. But
for some thoughts, like acknowledging past trauma or realizing something doesn't
quite fit with reality about how you've been thinking about some aspect of your
life, I do think it's important to welcome the thought paths to tea and take
them to their conclusion. In many ways that's what I'm doing now with this text.

## Moving the anchor

But here's the point, here's why we practice, here's the big shift that some may
call "awakening": instead of anchoring truth/reality on downstream thoughts, on
distorted reflections, on continuity stories, what if instead we anchor
truth/reality on direct input data of experience? Any thoughts, any distorted
reflections, are skeptically analyzed in the context of our baseline truth of
experience in this moment.

This can be a big deal. If you spent thirty years building a castle in your mind
to anchor your identity to, one that fully embodies what you are in the world,
and then suddenly you move the anchor of truth... the entire castle can come
crashing down. Stained glass shatters everywhere, towers crumble and burn, all
the art and beauty of the expression of your ego can simply dissolve and float
away. You may be left feeling empty, or extremely doubtful: why did I try so
hard? Was it all for nothing? Surely I'll go insane trying to live this way? Is
this just another scam that's going to suck me in and take advantage of me?
...I'd better get to repairing the walls.

Meditation does not say to stop building sand castles, it merely reminds us to
not be upset or surprised when the tide inevitably washes the castle away.

So the counter-intuitive realization here is that we don't have to "work" for
what we "want." Working and wanting both get re-contextualized in a way that
makes them look a bit silly. Applying "effort" means letting thoughts get in the
way; of course that will slow you down. Suffering is about wanting: the more you
want, the more you suffer, and the cycle continues.

# What about money?

You have to _work_ to live. In our backwards, unfair, and ridiculous system of
capitalism, there is no choice: you work hard to earn food and a living. There
is no other way, right? We could even imagine some ideal state of meritocracy,
where the most contributing get the most reward. Wouldn't that mean we should
work hard?

Much of the general insight applies here, but I think money and making a living
need some modern context.

1. Just because you have a lot of money doesn't mean others have less. There is
   enough money for everyone.
2. What if there existed in the world a job that you happily do because you feel
   something in your very bones that drives you to do that job? Would you feel
   shame in accepting large sums of money as payment for doing that job?
3. There is much to learn from the constant and ongoing suffering of the
   ultra-wealthy in our world. You can't buy yourself out of your own mental
   suffering. Trying to do so is a great way to reinforce the suffering.

Personally, I feel much the same about my twenty-plus-year career as a software
engineer as I did when I first started college: I have no idea what I want to do
or be in my life, but I generally like working with smart people who value
truth, science, and rational thinking, and who are funny, lighthearted, and fun
to work with.

At the financial height of my career, I was making about $330K as a senior
manager at a large company. Since that time I've self-imposed four pay cuts and
three demotions. I've landed around "Staff Software Engineer" at less than half
that.

At a certain level of maturity and insight, you stop trying to buy a rocket ship
and start looking for a good pair of walking shoes that fit well.

# Modern Life

After returning from a retreat, I heard something interesting: boredom is
becoming impossible with our smart media of the day, but meditation is the true,
permanent cure for boredom. This hit me when I was standing in line at the
grocery store and had the real urge to reach for my phone. Then I paused and
looked around. Really looked at what was happening in my experience and around
me. The flood of sensation was like watching a carnival. There was just so much
happening. It was impossible to keep track of, and endlessly entertaining,
enlightening, and engrossing. My next thought was to the tune of, "Why on earth
would I want to take out my phone?"

# Traps

Don't go spreading the good word of insight. If it arises naturally in
conversation, fine, but if you feel the need to shift your entire life into
being "the enlightened friend," you're simply building another mask, persona,
ego. Practice is not about "being" a meditator; it's about practicing, nothing
more. The rest happens without effort.

# Don't _just_ dance with it, DANCE with it!

When I was in college, I had a side job to help pay for food: office copier
delivery and setup. Machines were between $100K–500K, so this was no ordinary
delivery job. We would strap machines into the back of a truck, haul them up
ramps and elevators, and set them up for offices, then haul the old ones back to
the warehouse.

This was a two-person job, and my coworker was an older, burly, blue-collar
worker with little education and no real desire for one. He was a lifelong
delivery driver and seemed fairly content with that.

Most of the time he was reasonable and kind enough, but one delivery stuck with
me. Big, heavy copiers come on small, strong wheels, similar to your
refrigerator. Sometimes it's difficult to get those over sidewalk bumps,
especially when going up ramps. On this delivery I was pushing the copier when
it got stuck on one such bump. I was having difficulty and being careful so as
not to break anything. I was struggling for some time, trying to think of how to
get the thing to move in a professional way. I was fully lost in thought when my
coworker sighed and finally lost his temper. He walked over and yelled, "Well,
don't just dance with it, son!" He pushed me out of the way, rotated the copier
to get a better angle, and used some leverage with his leg to easily get it
moving. Then he took it all the way up while I watched helplessly from the side,
dejected, embarrassed, and feeling especially useless.

Now, in that moment I was pretty overwhelmed with emotion, but some deep,
conscious part of me got curious. I had a strange sense of something real and
important about what had just happened. This idea of wrestling in thought,
stalled out, spinning my wheels, making no progress, when I just needed to wake
up and "don't just dance with it," was a great lesson.

Later, in computer science, I would feel this mode come on. It would be late at
night in the lab, I'd be wrestling with a bug that I had no idea how to solve,
lost in my own complex web of functions and concepts I didn't quite grasp, not
knowing how to untangle things, and I'd know: re-running the same script is
going to make no real progress. I'm dancing with it.

Even later I would realize that when things like this happen, it's best to
either pause everything and go read about the aspect I'm least familiar with, or
to shelve the entire effort and come back to it later. More often than not, a
walk in nature or a long shower would reveal just what I need to stop dancing
with it and start making real progress.

I had a great manager who offered some insight into how my mind works when he
said, "We think the same: we build up and build up until we're sure we'll win."

This style of over-preparation can be a blessing or a curse. But what's more
important is that we act when the time calls for action, and that we make _real_
progress in those moments.

Again turning to video games: in many there is a main quest and then dozens,
hundreds, or even thousands of side quests. Some games are so full of side
quests that players are convinced they are the _real_ meat of the game.

But if we're focused on preparation or side quests for the wrong reasons, we can
miss out on life as it happens. Approached properly, side quests are actually a
hidden part of the main quest: a game character may need to level up their
strength or health to face the final boss, and that requires a side quest.

All too often we are caught up in thought or dreaming and not making real
progress.

I should say I take a little issue with the use of the term "dance" here. Life
is not a race where the one who gets to the end first wins; it's much more like
a dance, meant to be enjoyed at the pace at which it unfolds. So don't just
dance with it, half-hearted and stuck in your head. DANCE with it.

In meditation, folks often feel stuck, like they're just dancing with it and not
making real progress. Know that the stuck feeling itself is a pattern of
thought. Recognize it, accept it with an open heart, acknowledge it, and keep
practicing. Practicing is not about being stuck or moving forward. Practicing is
about practicing.
